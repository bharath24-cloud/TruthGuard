Text: ABSTRACT
Runs: R0(draw=0,fn=Times New Roman,fs=14.0,b=True,i=None)

--- P025 | align=JUSTIFY (3) | drawings=0 | len=2030 ---
Text: Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated extraordinary capabilities across conversational agents and generative text synthesis; however, their susceptibility to factual hallucinations—generating plausible yet factually unsubstantiated or demonstrably false assertions—presents severe reliability and safety risks in high-stakes domains such as healthcare, law, and engineering. Contemporary hallucination detection systems predominantly rely on monolithic LLM-as-a-judge prompts that introduce non-deterministic evaluation overhead, suffer from self-hallucination, conflate unverified assertions with direct contradictions, and mandate costly cloud dependencies. In this project, we engineer and implement TruthGuard, an explainable, locally executable Natural Language Processing framework designed for automated atomic claim decomposition, multi-source semantic evidence verification, and closed-loop evidence-grounded correction. TruthGuard operates via a modular multi-tier pipeline: input generation or manual response ingestion, atomic claim extraction with precise character-span tracking, dense semantic passage retrieval over private document libraries and the official FEVER Wikipedia corpus using Sentence-Transformers (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) and FAISS L2-normalized inner product indexing, natural language inference using the cross-encoder nli-deberta-v3-small, multi-evidence arbitration across seven distinct operational states, a mathematically bounded composite hallucination risk scoring formula, and grounded correction via local Ollama (qwen3:8b) with automated closed-loop re-verification. Experimental evaluation conducted on local hardware demonstrates 90.0% multi-class accuracy, 89.9% Macro F1, 100.0% retrieval recall@3, and sub-600ms latency on warm verification queries, while strategic CPU/GPU memory partitioning eliminates CUDA out-of-memory errors on 4GB VRAM mobile workstations. TruthGuard provides a zero-simulation, reproducible, and privacy-preserving safeguard for enterprise LLM deployment.

--- P037 | align=JUSTIFY (3) | drawings=0 | len=728 ---
Text: Over the past three years, the landscape of Artificial Intelligence and Natural Language Processing has been fundamentally transformed by the advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) based on the Transformer architecture. Modern autoregressive foundation models—such as Qwen, LLaMA, GPT-4, and Mistral—exhibit remarkable fluency, contextual reasoning, and zero-shot problem-solving competence across an expansive breadth of natural language tasks, including software synthesis, question answering, legal brief drafting, and medical dialogue summarization. However, despite their state-of-the-art linguistic fluency, autoregressive language models suffer from an inherent and pervasive vulnerability known as factual hallucination.
Runs: R0(draw=0,fn=Times New Roman,fs=12.0,b=None,i=None)

--- P038 | align=JUSTIFY (3) | drawings=0 | len=786 ---
Text: In contemporary Natural Language Generation (NLG) literature, factual hallucination is defined as the generation of statements that are either logically unfaithful to the provided source context (intrinsic hallucination) or factually untruthful and unsubstantiated with respect to established world knowledge (extrinsic hallucination). Because language models are trained via maximum likelihood estimation over next-token probability distributions, they prioritize semantic coherence and statistical likelihood over factual verifiability. Consequently, when encountering epistemic uncertainty or ambiguous training priors, models confabulate plausible-sounding falsehoods, invent synthetic citations, and state inaccurate dates or relationships with authoritative linguistic confidence.
Runs: R0(draw=0,fn=Times New Roman,fs=12.0,b=None,i=None)

--- P039 | align=JUSTIFY (3) | drawings=0 | len=662 ---
Text: This vulnerability poses critical risks in high-consequence enterprise environments. In clinical diagnostics, an unverified pharmaceutical claim could lead to adverse patient outcomes; in legal research, fabricated court precedents result in severe professional sanctions; and in customer-facing financial advisories, ungrounded figures incur substantial legal liabilities. Therefore, developing automated, rigorous, and explainable natural language verification systems that inspect, audit, and correct language model generations against authoritative reference knowledge bases has emerged as an essential priority for real-world artificial intelligence safety.
Runs: R0(draw=0,fn=Times New Roman,fs=12.0,b=None,i=None)

--- P040 | align=JUSTIFY (3) | drawings=0 | len=473 ---
Text: TruthGuard is conceived and engineered to resolve this challenge. TruthGuard is an explainable NLP framework developed to identify unsupported or fabricated claims in LLM responses, retrieve ground truth reference passages from structured and unstructured corpora, perform Natural Language Inference (NLI), provide grounded explainability rationales with exact provenance, and execute automatic evidence-grounded corrections without requiring costly cloud API dependencies.

--- P061 | align=None | drawings=0 | len=43 ---
Text: 2.1 Background of Fact Verification and NLI
Runs: R0(draw=0,fn=Times New Roman,fs=12.0,b=True,i=None)

--- P062 | align=JUSTIFY (3) | drawings=0 | len=500 ---
Text: The automated verification of natural language text against factual reference knowledge has evolved over three distinct algorithmic eras. Early fact-checking frameworks relied heavily on information extraction from curated Knowledge Graphs (e.g., Freebase, Wikidata) using symbolic triple matching (subject-predicate-object). While highly precise for structured biographical data, symbolic approaches proved incapable of handling complex, unstructured descriptive text or nuanced semantic assertions.
Runs: R0(draw=0,fn=Times New Roman,fs=12.0,b=None,i=None)

--- P063 | align=JUSTIFY (3) | drawings=0 | len=543 ---
Text: The second era was catalyzed by the development of Natural Language Inference (NLI) benchmarks, specifically the Stanford NLI (SNLI) and Multi-Genre NLI (MNLI) corpora. Formulated as a sequence classification task, NLI tasks a neural network with determining whether a given premise sentence entails, contradicts, or is neutral towards a hypothesis sentence. Initial deep learning models deployed Siamese LSTM or CNN encoders that mapped premise and hypothesis independently into dense vectors before calculating element-wise distance metrics.
Runs: R0(draw=0,fn=Times New Roman,fs=12.0,b=None,i=None)

--- P064 | align=JUSTIFY (3) | drawings=0 | len=662 ---
Text: The third and contemporary era is defined by pre-trained Transformer cross-encoders and dense vector retrieval systems within the Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) paradigm. In contrast to bi-encoder architectures that encode queries and documents independently, cross-encoders concatenate the premise and hypothesis into a single token sequence, permitting all-to-all cross-attention across all layers. Concurrently, dense semantic retrievers leveraging contrastive learning (such as Sentence-BERT and MiniLM) combined with inverted file indexing or flat inner product search (FAISS) have enabled sub-millisecond retrieval across millions of document chunks.
Runs: R0(draw=0,fn=Times New Roman,fs=12.0,b=None,i=None)

--- P067 | align=JUSTIFY (3) | drawings=0 | len=529 ---
Text: 1. Thorne et al. (2018) – FEVER Dataset: In their foundational EMNLP paper, Thorne et al. introduced the Fact Extraction and VERification (FEVER) dataset, consisting of 185,445 claims extracted from Wikipedia pages. Claims are categorized into SUPPORTS, REFUTES, or NOT ENOUGH INFO. Their baseline pipeline utilized TF-IDF for document retrieval followed by an MLP classifier, achieving an accuracy of 31.87% without ground truth passage hints. Their work established the formal standard for three-class fact-checking benchmarks.

Text: 2. Cross-Encoder Natural Language Inference:
